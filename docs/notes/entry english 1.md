# Trading Entry Methods: Aggressive, Mid-Conservative & Conservative

In the previous video I promised to make a special video on entry methods. Today we'll cover three types of entries — aggressive, mid-conservative, and conservative — along with the risk-management side of each: which entry method I personally use, and how each one is constructed.

Everyone knows these three types of entries, but *how* to actually execute them is what we'll discuss today, together with some important price-action logic.

## The General Question: Is Losing Easy?

Let's begin with a general statement and a general question — one that most (or all) of us have probably thought at some point: **Is it easy to lose in this market?**

Most people in the market are making losses. Everyone in this trading community knows the statistic: 95–96% of people lose money. The profit-making club is very small — the percentage is very low. So the majority of people make losses. If the maximum number of people are losing, then it follows that losing should be easy.

That leads to another common question: if someone is making a loss — a loss-making trade — what if you simply took the *opposite* trade at the same time? Suppose someone bought a call option or bought futures. You take your entry when they enter, and when they exit, you exit the opposite side. By copying a loss-making trader and doing exactly the opposite, wouldn't you profit?

This is a question everyone has probably asked at some phase of their trading life. If it's a loss-making trade, obviously it's a losing trade — so if you do the opposite, you should make money there. Statement-wise, that is completely true. But is it really? If everyone started doing this — catching loss-making traders and putting their opposite trades in larger quantity — they'd make more money, pull their losses out from there, and end up with higher profits. So is taking the opposite of loss-making trades a good idea for making money in the market?

The answer is **no**. Even if you take the opposite trade of a loss-making trade, you won't simply profit. Understand the psychology of why we're discussing this question today: listen to this statement carefully.

**As hard as it is to make a profit in this market, it is equally hard to consistently lose in this market.** If anyone could come here and consistently lose — or sustain consistent losses — then somehow, at some scale, that would become a profitable trade; that "loss" by itself would easily become a profitable trade. But that's not how it works.

It sounds like a funny statement — "since when did losing become hard in the market?" — but it's actually true. Think about your own trading journey, as all of you members have. From the very beginning of your trading journey, every day you've tried, one way or another, to find profitable trading setups. However much difficulty, struggle, frustration, and analysis it took to find those profitable setups, you'll face the exact same kind of difficulty when you go looking for loss-making setups.

You can test this right now, practically. Open your charts and try to find a *consistently* loss-making setup. If we could find one consistently loss-making setup, we could trade its opposite and make money in the market. But that's not the case. Whatever problems, issues, and struggles a retail or beginner trader faces in finding a profitable setup — learning from many places, doing dozens of courses, spending hours in the market — he'll face exactly the same kind of struggle trying to find a losing setup.

## The Three Entry Types

So when we talk about entries, today we'll categorize three types:

1. **Aggressive entry**
2. **Mid-conservative entry**
3. **Conservative entry**

We can compare them using a single parameter: **confirmation**. The aggressive entry is taken much *before* confirmation. The mid-conservative entry is taken a little *before* confirmation. The conservative (confirmatory) entry is taken *after* confirmation.

And this is a market fact: the later your entry, the more momentum you lose. The more confirmation you wait for, the more momentum loss. And the more momentum loss, the smaller your risk management becomes. The more conservative your entry, the smaller your risk management gets — but at the same time your stop loss becomes wider, the reliability of hitting your target drops, and the chances of losing on that particular trade increase. I'll explain all three through charts.

## Halt (Swing) Analysis Logic

In halt analysis, first we wait for a significant halt. The market made a buying swing, and here we saw one or two small red candles — a small retracement was created. In halt analysis, we wait for a *significant* halt — meaning a notable selling pressure should be seen in the market. So after this particular buying swing, the market halted at this particular point, from where a significant selling pressure appeared, or the market went slightly sideways. When two or three candles form in this significant selling, in halt analysis we mark a range.

The simple process for understanding entry is to follow halt analysis: after the buying swing, wherever the halt happens, we mark that as a **resistance level**.

The rule of halt analysis is this: if the market has created a buying swing, and afterwards it sustains at higher levels — it *holds* them — then there's a high probability of a buying-side breakout there, and a buying-side execution is done. Conversely, if a selling swing happens and after the selling swing there's a halt, the market *should* sustain the support level — and from there we take a selling-side trade.

Let me explain the logic of halt analysis clearly:

- If the market makes a buying swing right from the opening, and that buying swing *holds* — it tests higher levels — then from there the probability is high that a buying-side trade gets executed.
- Vice versa for the selling side. When does the selling swing get *confirmed*? Let's discuss confirmation first, and then compare the entries (aggressive, mid-conservative, and conservative) relative to that confirmation — where each one can be taken, and which one is called "aggressive" and which "confirmatory."

### When Confirmation Comes

The selling swing gets confirmed when: a selling swing appears, the market halts downward, tests, creates a lower high, and then breaks down. That breakdown is the confirmation of the selling swing.

There are two other patterns as well:

- If a buying swing was made but it *didn't* hold, and price fell to lower levels — and at those lower levels the market reverses the buying swing by more than **67%** (we're talking about the previous swing here, i.e., the morning swing) — then that itself is a confirmation that the market has turned selling-side. We would no longer say "the strength is still there." When a buying swing reverses by more than 67%, we say today's market has turned selling-side. At that point we don't wait for holding, or testing, or anything — directly, if the buying swing has reversed, that itself is the confirmation that no strength remains in the market.
- The vice versa: if there was a selling swing first and the buying side reverses, that itself is the confirmation that strength has come into the market.

So in halt analysis, strength or weakness in the market, and its direction, are determined on the basis of these cases.

### Conservative (Confirmatory) Entry

Let's now work backwards from that confirmation point and understand the three entry types.

The conservative entry is *always* at confirmation — when the full confirmation arrives that the breakout has happened. According to halt analysis, here the proper confirmation came: the breakout happened. The market had strength — price held the buying swing. How was the strength confirmed today? When price held at higher levels — from that, confirmation came that price has strength. After the strength, the breakout came — the breakout *was* the confirmation.

So the last (most conservative) entry is taken at the breakout. In the confirmatory entry, the entry happens here: we enter on the closing candle, our stop loss is placed here, and after that we hold the trade. Since the momentum is continuous, we book a good amount of profit — a good reward ratio in this particular trade.

### Mid-Conservative Entry

The second type of entry is the **mid-conservative entry**. It's always taken *before* confirmation — when confirmation is about to come, and a little strength starts to appear, that's where the mid-conservative entry is planned.

Let me state it in this same example. Where would the confirmatory entry be? After the buying-swing creation, first we wait for a halt — we check whether this particular buying swing is reversing or not. Only after one higher-low creation do we plan the mid-conservative entry here.

At this particular point, the market established support; after that the swing came. After this buying swing, there was this particular retracement. When, after the retracement, the market starts recovering again — that's where the mid-conservative entry is planned. So, mapping the mid-conservative entry, it would be here: a proper low was created, a buying swing came, price reversed from the selling side, and again we see the reversal on the buying side.

That's exactly what happened on this day — my trade was planned here, and the execution was done at this point: the buying position was created on the basis of the mid-conservative entry. When the reversal happened — you can see in this candle the selling swing was reversed — so after the closing, from here, this particular buying-side trade was planned.

### Aggressive Entry

Now, what is the **aggressive entry**? It's the most preferable entry. It's used when there's security — when the overall confirmation of major market levels is already in line. Or it can even be used in general cases.

The aggressive entry has multiple benefits:
- A good risk-reward ratio.
- A much smaller stop loss.
- Entry at the best possible price.

When do we use the aggressive entry method? When the overall view is already confirmed — like here. If we look at the overall view, it's buying-side: the market broke down a major resistance level with a successful closing, and after the closing there was a gap-up opening. The buying swing is in place, so strength is confirmed. The overall overview is confirmed, and there's a high probability of a continuous pattern. So here we can directly line up the aggressive entry.

How is the aggressive entry taken? It's taken **just on the support creation of the first retracement**. So the buying swing was created, a halt happened. We simply observe whether price creates a selling swing here. As soon as the selling swing's support formation happens — the moment support is made — a buying-side entry is executed directly.

Many times the aggressive entry is taken on a proper closing — like this particular candle: when this candle closed on the buying side, the entry was taken on the closing. That's an aggressive entry. Other times, the aggressive entry is planned *before* this green candle even closes — when price took support, started reversing, and came back above the opening price, that's also when the entry is planned.

So the aggressive entry is taken early and aggressively, but it carries a lot of plus points by itself. If you take the confirmatory entry instead — say the entry was planned here — the market could have retraced from here or sold off, and our stop loss would have been hit in the market.

### The Major Difference: Conservative vs. Aggressive

If we discuss the major difference between the conservative and the aggressive/confirmatory entry methods — what's the biggest benefit? It's this, and I always say it: **professional execution** is called professional only when your stop loss gets hit because the market's *condition* has changed.

On any given day, you can call yourself a professional trader only if your stop loss was not hit due to market noise or small fluctuations, and was not hit due to normal volatility and retracement. Your stop loss should get hit only when the market's condition itself has changed — for example, the scenario was bullish and now a bearish confirmation has come. If your stop loss hits only then, that's a perfect entry. An entry where your stop loss hits only because the market's condition changed — that's what a best entry, or professional execution, means.

Look at this example: if a confirmatory entry was taken, what would happen today? It's not necessary that the market continues after the breakout. If the market retraced after the breakout — even a little — our stop loss would have gone. But in the aggressive entry, our entry happened at the point *from where momentum came*. We took the entry after support was made — we didn't take it during the breakout, we took it while support was being formed.

I mentioned this in another video too: the entry should be such that where your execution is, from there the chart should give you 10–15 points of momentum (in option premium terms). So in the aggressive entry, we take the trade on the support being made, and a general buying swing is expected from there. If the entry is always like this — some points of momentum coming right after entry — then automatically the stop loss becomes safe. The market gave a general signal, gave a little momentum after the entry, price went up, the position came into profit immediately, and it moved far away from the stop loss. Automatically, the position becomes safe.

That's why in the aggressive entry, the key point is: take it after support is made, or during the formation — so that momentum comes and the stop loss becomes safe.

In this particular condition, what would your stop loss be in the aggressive entry? If the market's confirmation came and now a reversal happens from here, when does our stop loss trigger? It triggers on a **support breakdown**. And if this particular support breaks, you can all say it in the comment section: the buying-side view becomes wrong. So in the aggressive entry, the stop loss triggers when the buying-side view itself has changed — when the market couldn't hold the higher level and broke down from support.

These are the inherent benefits of the aggressive entry.

## Second Scenario: Selling-Side Aggressive Entry

Let's look at another scenario. If we look at the previous day — this is the second type of scenario. Both cases will happen: one is a buying-side strength confirmation (a buying-swing confirmation), and now we'll look at a selling-swing strength confirmation.

Where did the market halt? It halted here. A selling swing came, the market first halted here, but there was only a one-candle retracement and then a breakdown again. So what do we do? The halt comes here — now this is a *significant* halt. After the halt, what's done? A buying or selling position is created. The aggressive-side position is aligned with the major overview — like what happened here: a major-level breakout occurred, the market gaped up, and it's testing support levels.

So in the aggressive entry, it's possible to plan entries on *both* sides:

- If we plan the **selling side**: the selling-side entry happens when price made this selling swing and halted, and after that there's a retracement. In this retracement, when a resistance forms — like the resistance taken here — after resistance is taken, when price goes below the opening price, this closing came (this selling candle). When it starts falling, that's where the aggressive selling entry is made. The stop loss goes on the high. Why? Because the market hasn't tested the intraday low, so the confirmation of the selling trade hasn't come yet.

- So if we line up the aggressive selling-side entry, a stop loss is set. After that, we again go aggressive — but on the **buying side**. Where would the buying-side entry be? A reversal happened again — now the selling swing reversed and support hasn't been tested, so from here the buying-side confirmation will come. So for this particular retracement — as soon as a support formation happens — after that the entry is planned again. This is the support formation; here our closing came, so here again we'll have our aggressive buying-side entry, after the confirmation.

So that's the picture. When your aggressive entry is wrong — suppose the overall overview/analysis was there and we planned the aggressive selling-side trade — then one stop loss goes, and the second (buying-side) target comes on confirmation. But if from aggressive itself you had planned the buying side, then consider: this is the selling swing, and after the halt, after the selling swing's retracement — since we're taking a buying-side trade — when does the buying-side trade happen? It happens after the selling-side retracement, when support starts forming. Here, after the first support, this first red candle is the selling-side retracement — that will be our support level. After the support level, see this particular green candle — it reversed more than 80% — so on its closing, our entry happens right here. And around this candle's low, the stop loss is placed.

And again, in this aggressive entry, when does our stop loss trigger? If the market breaks this particular low, what does it mean? The market isn't testing support — the bullish view became wrong. And recall what I told you about halt analysis confirmation: the selling side gets confirmed when it makes a lower high and tests support. So if the view itself became wrong, when should the stop loss trigger? Exactly when the view changes. The bullish view holds when the market tests support and sustains from the higher level. If price reverses instead — it came only to test support and then turned down — the view itself became selling-side, and holding the buying-side trade has no logic anymore. So the exit is executed here.

But as we continued on the buying side, here again we'd see a good target and momentum. In this trade, if we look at the confirmatory entry: this is the intraday high — when the intraday high breaks out, our entry is planned on the closing here. So that's how much difference there is between the confirmatory entry and the aggressive entry.

### The Break-Even Exit Benefit

Viewers who watch regularly may have observed that many times we also get a chance where we take an entry and the momentum comes right after the entry — like here. In this case, suppose you had lined up the selling-side trade. Let's take this as an example and discuss it:

In the market, a buying swing was created, and here our halt came. Where would your aggressive entry be planned? On the support creation of the first retracement. This is the support creation — the retracement (selling-side) came, and now support was created. So here our entry is lined up, around this red line, with the stop loss kept with the swing.

Many such cases happen. The benefit of the aggressive entry — which I personally use — is that if momentum comes from here, and after the momentum the market breaks out and then fails — like here, after confirmation the market is failing — we can execute our position at **break-even** and reverse to the selling side. So this is also a benefit/opportunity of the aggressive entry: if momentum comes right after the aggressive entry, we don't make a loss — we get the opportunity to exit at break-even.

## Summary: Aggressive Entry Rules in Halt Analysis

We've discussed these examples. Now let me brief the entry points once more.

In halt analysis, see how the aggressive entry is planned:

1. First, we **wait for the halt**.
2. After the halt, if the entry is an aggressive *buying-side* entry: first a buying swing comes, then a halt, then a selling swing. So in the buying-swing case, the first retracement (of the selling swing) is the retracement we want — **directly on the support being made, we buy**.
3. If it's a *selling swing*: after the halt, obviously a buying swing will come first. After that buying swing, a retracement comes. But that first retracement belongs to the buying swing — so we **don't** take the entry there. We take the entry only after the selling swing, on the resistance formation of its retracement.

So the major difference in the aggressive entry method is: in a buying swing, you take the entry on the first retracement; in a selling swing, you skip the buying-swing retracement and enter only after the selling swing's own retracement.
