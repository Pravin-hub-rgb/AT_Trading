## Basic market truth: nothing moves in a straight line forever

Nobody has ever seen a market go up and just keep going up forever without any pullback. If the market is going to go up, how will it get there? Maybe for a day or two it looks like a straight line — even a 45-degree trending close — but these are exception cases. There might be one or two such trading sessions in a year. Nothing is 100%. But the *general* nature is different.

The general nature is: if the market is going up, it will face resistance, weakness will show up, and the market will correct. Then if it needs to go higher, after correcting, new buyers come in and buy, and the market proceeds upward again. The same logic applies to downtrends — the market will show buying, correct slightly, and continue downward. This is the market's most special characteristic, and everything in these sessions will be built on this foundation.

### Multiple swings before final high

The market doesn't go from point A to point B in one move. It typically makes **two or three swings** before reaching its final high. The same applies to selling swings — the market will make multiple swings down before reaching its final low. Understanding this helps you stay patient during a trade: just because the market retraces doesn't mean your swing is invalid — it may simply be building the next leg of the move.

## Only two kinds of trading days

So here's the core simplification: there are really only two types of days —

1. **Continuation** — the market keeps going in the direction it started.
2. **Reversal** — the market flips direction instead.

Whether it's Indian markets or any market, going by how we structure our timing, there are exactly these two kinds of trading sessions, and these are the two we need to learn to trade. Either the day becomes a continuation case, or a reversal case. Nothing else gets created outside of these two.

### How to identify reversal vs continuation

After the market falls and then bounces back up, the key question is: **how strong is the bounce?**

- **Weak bounce** (small buying candles, not much follow-through) → this is a **retracement** — the selling will likely continue. This is a selling opportunity: enter on the next candle after the weak bounce, with a stop above the bounce high.
- **Strong bounce** (big buying candles, clear momentum) → this is a **reversal** — the market has shifted direction. Do NOT sell here. Wait for the reversal to complete and then look for buying opportunities.

The same logic applies in reverse after a rally: weak pullback = continuation (buying opportunity); strong pullback = reversal (don't buy).

## The core method: waiting for "10 o'clock," identifying the swing, and entering on retracement

Here's the base method, step by step:

1. **Wait roughly until 10:00 AM.** To be clear — this isn't about staring at the clock and acting at the literal, exact second of 10:00. It's a *round figure* reference — could be ten minutes earlier or later. The point is to let the market settle first.
2. **After that, there are only two outcomes** — either the swing that's built up continues, or weakness shows up and it reverses. The same logic applies whether it's a buying swing or a selling swing: after ~10 AM, either continuation or reversal happens.
3. **Mark the ~10 AM candle** so you can clearly observe from there. From that point, whichever direction the swing is going, you wait for a **retracement**. Once the retracement comes and a fresh support (or resistance) forms, that's where you look to enter — in the direction of the original swing.
4. Importantly, a **single candle poking against the level is not treated as a valid retracement.** It needs to be a real, visible pullback — multiple candles retracing, not just one candle touching the level and bouncing. A single candle "poke" is noise; multiple candles retracing is signal.
5. There's also a simpler, complementary way to look at the same idea via the **closing session** (roughly 12:00–2:00 PM, sometimes momentum shows even a bit later): if the market has moved down, look at where support is holding into that closing window; if it's moved up, look at where resistance is holding. Keep it simple — the fewer variables you track, the easier it gets, especially starting out. So early on: just watch the closing session (12–2 PM) and see what the market is doing there. **Next-day bias:** if the market falls and holds selling pressure into the 12–2 PM window, that's generally a negative close — the next day can be expected to test lower levels. If the market rises and holds buying into 12–2 PM, that's a positive close — the next day can be expected to test higher levels. Momentum can sometimes extend a bit beyond 2 PM if the move is strong.
6. **Underlying logic**: price can't move in one direction forever — it must retrace at some point. So we wait for the retracement, wait for support/resistance to re-form after it, and enter there.

### Option strike price selection

Use **ATM (at-the-money)** or the nearest **ITM (in-the-money)** strike price. Avoid far OTM (out-of-the-money) — they may look cheap but have poor liquidity and widen quickly. Prefer **weekly expiry** for better liquidity and tighter spreads.

### Entry timing — no trades before 9:45 AM

**No trades before 9:45 AM.** The first few minutes after market open are pure noise — the market is still finding its direction. Especially during turbulent or gap-up/gap-down openings, wait until at least 9:45 before taking any position. This is a hard rule, not a suggestion.

### Position sizing — start with 2–3 lots

Start with **2–3 lots maximum** when beginning. Only add lots as your account grows and your consistency improves. This limits risk while you're still learning the method. As your account and confidence grow, you can gradually increase position size — but always proportionally to account size, never impulsively.

## Exit strategy — stay in until the swing completes or reverses

When you're in a trade and the market moves in your favor, the key is patience — stay in the trade until the swing completes or reverses. Don't exit prematurely just because the position is in profit. However, always keep a stop-loss in place, even when the trade is working in your favor. Examples from the backtest: on the 29th, entry was taken and the market moved favorably — the instruction was to stay in until the swing ends. On the 21st, similar logic applied — hold until the swing shows signs of completion or reversal.

## Stop-loss placement method

For **buying trades**: place the stop-loss below the last swing low. For **selling trades**: place the stop-loss above the last swing high. The number of candles that form the swing determines the stop distance — more candles = wider stop. If the swing is tight (few candles), the stop is closer; if the swing is wide (many candles), the stop is farther. This is a mechanical rule — don't guess, measure from the actual swing structure on the chart.

## Option selling — success rate vs risk-reward tradeoff

Option selling only at confirmed support/resistance levels gives roughly an **80% success rate**. But if you sell at any candle without confirmation, success drops to **50–60%**. Here's the tradeoff: higher success rate requires wider stop-losses, which means more risk per trade. So even with an 80% win rate, risk management is critical — position sizing and stop discipline determine overall profitability. This is why starting small (2–3 lots) and scaling up only as your account grows is strongly recommended.
