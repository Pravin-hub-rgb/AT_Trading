## Opening & recap

Good evening, everyone. In the last session we discussed the N/M patterns, the "two-legs" setup, and the market's domination. Today we build further on the **execution** side — specifically how to take a **small stop-loss with a bigger target**, and what challenges come with it.

### Why "N/M"? Just an easy label

Saying "buyer domination till the highs, reverse to the lows, back to the highs" every time is clunky — so the shorthand N/M is used. It may sound like beginner stuff ("W pattern, N pattern"), but it is kept deliberately easy to understand. It's a **universal concept applicable on each and every timeframe** — big swings, small swings, even within a single candle's observation. What worked on large frames works identically on small ones; the same observation done large-scale is reused at small scale for confirmation.

### Every concept combines into a single execution

Consistency cannot develop from a single isolated concept — if you don't hold the full overview, no single concept alone builds consistency. Everything discussed so far — base concept, swings, overview, domination — gets used together **inside every single execution**. Today's examples may look like things already shown in videos, but without the full chain of concepts, they can't be replicated consistently.

## The challenges with quick, momentum-based execution

To trade like this, a few things must be true:

1. **Your execution has to be quick.** Timing matters. If you are slow, the risk management won't hold.
2. **Don't enter before the momentum** actually starts. If you enter too early (in the "parked" phase), the market may not move — the stop-loss gets hit. Entering early is not a *safe* entry.
3. **The professional entry is "getting on a moving train and getting off a moving train."** You enter while the market moves in the momentum — and exit while it's still moving. If you sit in a "parked car" (sideways), nothing happens and you are better off staying out.

So the ideal entry is one where the market supplies the momentum right after you enter. The stop is small, so **the momentum has to come quickly** to keep the trade safe.

## Average candle size & realistic targets

Because the stop is small, the target must line up with the session. The basis is the **average candle size** — the average range/height of the session's candles:

- Measure the average candle size (sum of highs-lows / number of candles), or estimate by the general candle size and take a slightly larger-than-average reference.
- Since the SL is placed very near the low, the base criterion for expected momentum is at least one average candle / a few points of move. Because the SL is small, an entry at a good spot sees the market jump 5–10 points quickly — that jump makes the SL automatically safe.
- **Don't set the SL too big** — then the target ratio becomes big and the entry gets sloppy, and in many cases the target is missed. Keep the SL small and the target realistic.

### The 5-point immediate-momentum rule (most important point)

Plan the SL by average candle size. **After your entry, roughly 5 points of momentum should arrive almost immediately** — in maximum cases this should happen. If it does, the small SL is instantly protected. This is THE most important checkpoint of this system. If that quick push doesn't come, the entry needs improvement.

### The key entry rule

Small SL + small target = the entry must be precise. Without immediate follow-through, you can't run a small-SL, small-target system.

## Two execution scenarios: flow-favor vs flow-against

This is today's core structural distinction:

### Scenario A — Execution IN favor of the flow

When the market dips WITHIN your favorable direction (e.g., a buying trend, market retraces): **no N/M check needed.** As soon as support identifies via the pace-of-momentum break, execute directly on the buying side. Simple — "the break came, we execute."

### Scenario B — Execution AGAINST the flow

When selling INTO a buying flow (retracement expected from higher levels): this needs a **confirmation entry** — minimum **two rejections**, with execution possible on the third. Because in a buying flow, once a buying swing forms, continuous reversal won't happen — so domination must be proven first.

### Morning-session preference

He personally prefers morning-hour entries because that's where the best momentum lives ("momentum comes in the morning; maximum time good momentum is found"). You can also trade per the overview later (it won't fall continuously — wherever support forms, a bounce back comes), but the visible morning opportunity is itself high-probability: weakness building → minimum expectation = intraday-low breakdown → execute.

### Counter-scenario handling

If instead of retracing, the market runs to the highs with weakness building: plan the RETRACEMENT trade there instead — same logic, same two-rejection + third-confirmation execution, just at the high rather than the low. And if direction is with-flow: simply wait for support + confirmation → execute.

## Timing rules

- **No entry before 9:45.** Entries happen only after 9:45 — he is not recommending anyone skip a case, but skip any setup that appears before 9:45. Before 9:45, execution must not be done in any condition; it's a standard rule — "before 9:45 nothing happens anyway."
- **Avoid entries after ~11:12.** For morning-session trades, avoid entries after ~11:12; absolutely nothing after 12 — before that is acceptable.
- **10 AM = the formation window.** Even picking charts blind, the market's first swing / its support or resistance forms **around 10 o'clock**. He demonstrated by marking the 10-o'clock candle across many dated charts (some slightly after 10, some exactly at 10, occasionally earlier; the 27th's buying swing marked there too). Multiple examples all form their first S/R near the 10 o'clock candle. **Timing is crucial** — keep it at the back of your mind: the level isn't formed yet; it will form in a couple of candles, maybe after breaking lows first — but it WILL form there. Whether continuation or reversal follows that area is secondary — observe and plan accordingly. Strength → price goes near the high (doesn't reach the lows); weakness → near the lows → confirmation.

### Why the market behaves this way

The market's nature: after opening, it runs 5–8–10 candles in one direction, then resists, then plans direction. Five-minute candles × 5–6–8–10 ≈ the 10 o'clock round figure (after 9:45). We ADAPT ourselves to the market's rhythm — we don't drag the market to our schedule; we position ourselves according to how the market moves. If every day opened with huge unidirectional swings, everyone would short every dip and buy every rise — the market can't function that way. So uncertainty comes first (fall a bit, reverse, rise, resist), and the decisive flow comes after — and the support/resistance forms inside exactly that window. The market has opened at 9:15 for years; this observation is universal, not new.

**Generic day anatomy** (taught alongside): open → up first → down → retrace-back close; on the small timeframe: open → high → then low → pullback close; later-half momentum takes support and pushes to higher levels. A gap-down variant: a buying-flow market gaps down, builds support, climbs into a higher swing — hence "it won't keep falling."

Working WITH these favorable factors (direction favor + timing favor) is why identification succeeds maximum time; going against them creates messes. Timing + the break together raise the probability of where/what will happen.

## The first swing is never traded inside

Looking at his maximum entries: he does NOT execute in the direction of the first swing's continuation. Wait for the first swing to complete (it rarely exceeds 4–5–6 candles; max-to-max 5–6 per yesterday's tested charts; absolute upper bound 8–10), THEN take either its continuation or its reversal. Entering mid-first-swing is how people get chopped.

Does the first swing ever continue hugely? Generally NO — the class confirmed it in chorus. Only 1–2 exceptional days exist (and even March 1's runner resisted along the way; its morning session "slipped out of hand"). Back-of-mind certainty: the market WILL resist; that certainty is your edge.

## Pace of momentum break — the identification engine

### Definition

Observe the NATURE of the first swing the market creates (selling side or buying side): what kind of selling/buying activity, what pattern, what speed. Wherever that established pattern turns *unusual* — different speed or different swing behavior — that's the **pace-of-momentum break**, and it's the highest-probability location for support/resistance formation.

Classification nuance: if the first green candle is immediately fully consumed and its low broken, NEGLECT it and classify the swing as a selling swing. Within a swing, note its characteristic recovery pattern (e.g., single-candle recoveries); that nature CONTINUES across swings — and an exceptional deviation in nature IS the break identification, i.e., the market creating support or resistance.

### THREE types of pattern breaks

The pace-of-momentum break occurs in three ways:

1. **Candle-wise pattern break:** the established up/down rhythm breaks (e.g., alternating 1-green-1-red suddenly becomes 3 consecutive selling candles).
2. **Domination-wise break:** an aggressive domination candle consumes/reverses the swing sharply. (Aggressive buying/selling can also be counted under domination.)
3. **Candle-size/aggressive break:** a candle far larger than the session's average appears — unusual size itself signals the break.

### Reading the swing rhythm lets you predict the next candle

If the buying swing's rhythm is "2–3 greens up, 1 red rejection (seller dominates one candle), greens resume breaking highs," then you KNOW the next red will likely be absorbed and highs broken again. No action on single reds — the pattern hasn't broken. The break arrives only via: 2–3 sideways candles / lows breaking after a high, OR one big domination red (big size versus previous candles). Until then: wait. And the break WILL come — "the break will definitely come" — it's market nature; in any intraday session's first swing this is maximum-time behavior, so we simply wait for that session.

### Confirmation needs follow-through

One big candle alone isn't a confirmed break. Example: a large candle printed at the crucial timing — big size versus previous results — but it consumed nothing, and the next candle gave no continuation → wait for more confirmation. When continuation finally resumed — big selling reversing the market after support, a domination candle closing, and for the first time upward continuation — THAT is the confirmed pattern break. Big candle + close + follow-through continuation = confirmed break.

### First break = highest probability spot

Wherever the FIRST break appears is where S/R most probably forms. Today (5th March): the market fell, a green candle came, and for the first time the next candle's seller dominated but FAILED to break the lows — the low breakdown didn't happen, buyers reversed it again. Pattern broken; confirmation arrived within just two candles; the third candle took support and broke the highs — apply the general high-low-break concept, continuation confirmed, "the train has started moving." Flow-favor → execute directly on the pace-of-momentum break — that was the 4th March buying-side execution plan.

### Market nature is the only certainty — hence universal

Nothing in markets is certain EXCEPT the nature: price cannot travel one direction forever. That holds on the major overview, on the smallest timeframe, and inside the intraday first swing alike. Exceptional days exist, but that's not the nature. Trading THAT nature in every case is what makes the concept universal. The higher price extends, the bigger the probability that it resists — it will resist, stay sideways a while; whether it then continues up or falls, the resistance itself is certain. That certainty is the edge.

### Point-to-point identical identification

Follow the rules and everyone identifies the SAME point — no member will identify earlier or elsewhere. "If it came here, it came here" — no confusion, point to point.

## The two types of entries

There are two entry situations:

1. **Type 1 (easy to identify):** the market makes a high, and then the selling domination forms **below** the high — creating a lower-high / lower-low common pattern. Once that low breaks with domination, you act.
2. **Type 2:** the high breaks one by one (only one-two candles against), but the domination is on the selling side — a slower, weaker-pace case needing confirmation from a clean pattern-break candle. (The demonstrated counter-flow short was this second type — a buying-swing case.)

Related — two types of weakness/selling build-up: (a) sell → market falls → clean continuation; (b) weakness building BUT the market keeps breaking each candle's high — up, sold, unsustainable, new high, sold again; domination without a lower-high/lower-low structure. Type (b) is analyzed via the option chart, volume, and price strength.

## Confirmation via rejection & "third rejection"

- When the market approaches and **rejects** a level repeatedly (signals of selling/weakness), the confirmation builds after proper domination and rejection.
- **Two rejections minimum; execute on the third.** Sequence: buyer bounce → selling from the high = rejection 1 → market to lower levels (weakness builds) → buyer pushes → selling again = rejection 2 → buyer pushes AGAIN but cannot reach the previous highs = additional weakness sign → when it starts falling on the third attempt, "the train has started moving."
- **What the third rejection confirms:** the third rejection proves the market can't even reach the prior high before rolling over; the probability of opposing IT also collapses. That's the entry trigger.
- **If no third rejection comes:** entry then forms on the clean pattern-break/domination candle — arriving momentum is itself price's self-confirmation.
- **Flow-exception on rejections:** if the overview is selling and the market tests a major LOW (e.g., March 1), that test is AGAINST the selling direction — i.e., in the trader's favor — so a direct call is allowed WITHOUT waiting for three rejections. But when selling AT higher levels after a major-high test, the three-rejection check applies.

**Meta-rule:** once you're in a trade, **follow your plan** — don't change it mid-trade. If you've decided "no action before 1:2 / exit after 1:3," do exactly that, even if price runs past and then reverses.

## Volume & option-strike confirmation

### General volume signature of a session

Volume spikes at open (the first one-two general candles carry volume — nature of markets) → reduces through mid-session → **the BREAK of that reduction marks where domination occurs** (in maximum cases, very many cases) → spikes again at close. Watch for the mid-day volume-break. Option charts can differ slightly (positions execute there), but the general signature holds; futures or option charts may both be observed.

- When the market falls, a **significant/double volume increase** in the option confirms a confident move — where proper domination comes, double volume is generally seen.
- If the market falls but **volume does not increase**, it's a **divergence / not a confident move** — take extra confirmation before executing such a case.
- **Big volume ON the support-identification candle is itself a confirmation** — a huge volume printing in that candle confirms in itself.

### Divergence reading

Divergence = spot breaks highs but the option doesn't follow; or the option sags to lows / sustains lows early while spot holds — extra weakness/strength information. In many cases divergence appears BEFORE spot reverses — which lets you "execute even more confidently." Conversely: spot breaks lows but the option stops breaking and buying starts early = a buying-side confirmation.

### Which chart for volume?

For volumes, watch OPTIONS (not futures) when executing in options. For stocks, the futures chart is the norm — stock-option traders watch futures volume. But in Nifty, he watches the OPTION STRIKE's own volume. Futures remain usable for general direction; both may be watched — personal choice. (Futures are even better for analysis; both can be watched.)

### Strike selection via volume break

Check ATM + ITM, roughly 1–2–3 strikes: usually only ONE shows the volume break/divergence while the others don't. Strikes normally run nearly identical, so a difference matters — **the strike showing the volume break is the strongest; prefer it.** It carries its own distinct comparative divergence.

### Don't over-deepen (course-format note)

As price rises, option-strike behavior gives further weakness identifications (whether it will sustain) — but there's no need to go that deep, and there are format limitations: this is a 6-day course, planned at an hour a day, and things expand as they're discussed. The aim is maximum concepts with clarity, so no points get missed.

## Risk management — reversal rules

- **Momentum beyond 1:2 then reversing → exit near cost:** if momentum has traveled beyond 1:2 (target being 1:3) and the trade reverses, don't panic-exit instantly and don't let it hit the SL — try to exit early on the reversal, or cut cost-to-cost when price comes near cost. That's the risk-management rule.
- **Cost-to-cost exit unlocks a second trade:** if the first trade exits cost-to-cost, a SECOND trade may be executed, with fresh direction planning.

### Advanced techniques — NOT advised yet

- **Early exit on weak reversal (before 1:2):** exiting before 1:2 on anticipation requires strong confirmations and perfection — only after practice builds it. Not advised at the beginning stage. Premature exits that get followed by the intended move create pressure and regret and **disrupt the mindset** — avoid for now.
- **Support-break cut-and-re-enter:** with experience: entry taken on support, bounce back comes, and if the bounce resists and a reversal looks successfully sufficient from a good entry point — cut the position right there, because it's about to break the support, make a NEW support, and you'll take it again there. Skill-based; not for beginners.

## Order types & exits

- **Entry orders:** use LIMIT creatively, almost like a market order — e.g., to buy, place a limit ABOVE the current price (buy-stop-style: if price crosses that level, it buys), or place a bargain limit below so the position fills only down there. Orders used: LIMIT, SL, and SL-M (stop-loss-limit). Caveat: with a true limit order, if price never retraces to your limit, no fill happens. Small quantity → plain MARKET order is fine, no problem. Protection via SL / SL-M.
- **Exit:** simple — you can plot targets, but he prefers NOT plotting targets; he exits manually on market order so trades can breathe.

## Retracement-expectation logic (the short case)

A general retracement was expected: the market, after building support, ran continuously from lower levels toward the highs — it won't go continuously. Somewhere around 10 o'clock the pattern break comes; at the break the market RESISTS, and resisting, it RETRACES. Minimum expectation: at least a support break and some pullback. Plan both outcomes from the entry: if it reverses [against you] → cost-to-cost exit; otherwise plan the second trade from there.

### Big swings shrink reversal probability

The strength-swing case: the market made a continuously big swing holding higher lows; a retracement came, but the swing's size became very large. Whenever one-directional momentum gets that big, the probability of it reversing all the way back to the lows — of selling domination — automatically reduces. Size tempers expectations. The pattern-break observation still applies to find where it does turn.
