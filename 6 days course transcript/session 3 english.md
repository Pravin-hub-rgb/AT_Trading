## The market has limited possibilities

First, let's look at what possibilities the market even has. We generally see the market open, go down sometimes, go up sometimes, come back down after going up, sometimes make higher lows and sometimes lower highs. So here's the key: **price has a limited set of possibilities.**

Why limited? Because in any trading session:
- **Time is limited** — the Indian market has a specific trading window. Even if it extended, a certain fixed duration remains.
- Because activity is time-limited, the **kinds of cases** that can get created are also limited. If the market traded much longer, more of the same-type swings might form — but the **base nature stays the same**.

That base nature: after any swing is created, price either **proceeds toward the swing's bottom level**, or it **proceeds to a higher level** — i.e., it goes down to a lower level, or it retraces, holds higher, and goes further up.

Why is it uncertain? Because if the market simply ran in a straight line, everybody would make money — that's not the market's behavior. It stays **uncertain, unexpected, and unpredictable** — all three. Unless the world economy and the market's timing change drastically, no huge exceptional change is going to happen from here.

### The confidence this gives
So you should never be sitting at your charts feeling "what is happening? where is the market going?" That happens when your **probabilities aren't pre-decided**. If everything is pre-decided — if every scenario and probability is analyzed in advance ("after any swing the market can go this way or that") — then when the market starts doing exactly one of those possibilities, you gain **confidence in execution**. You know: "this is happening now, so from this point price can go this far." And nothing else can happen besides the limited set. That's how confidence comes in execution.

## The two/three types of swings

In the last session we discussed the first swing — it makes 5-6 candles on one side. But two types of buying swings exist; the pace of momentum need not always be high. A buying swing can be sharp, or it can be slower. And it doesn't matter what the pace of momentum is — the swing marking is done on the basis of the swing itself.

Concretely, a buying swing either:
1. Creates a **resistance** and **sustains it** (a strong swing that keeps holding higher), or
2. **Retraces** — going up, then pulling back to support (a weaker pace / retracement type).

These two types are based on the **pace of momentum**.

**Identify the pace of momentum first.** If a swing is built in a very slow/weak way after ~10 AM, be a little careful with your entry — a bit more cautious execution. If the pace of momentum is much higher / more aggressive, there will be different methods and different expectations for the intraday target.

Also, don't rush to call something on the basis of a single red candle. Example: the market made a buying swing, retraced (a red candle), then came back up — so the pace of momentum of that buying swing is different from what a single red candle would suggest. If you misjudge it in a hurry, you'd be wrong.

### Single-candle rule
A market move can be created from one, two, or three candles — but what you must observe is whether, after opening, the market **first went down or first went up**. So:
- If the market keeps making those (four, five, six) candles in one direction and **sustains with momentum**, that direction is the swing.
- A **single candle poking against the level is not treated as a valid swing** unless it's followed by confirmation (a real retracement).
- If the first candle was a red (selling) candle and it **sustains** with momentum, it's a selling swing. If a small doji or small candle forms instead, it's not a confirmed swing yet.

## Strength vs. weakness — where to act

As we discussed, when making an entry the very first thing that should hit your mind based on the overview is the expectation:

- If the market is moving **up** on any day → the overview expectation is that it will form a **resistance** and retrace. But that's not guaranteed — it can instead be a **continuation** case.
- If the market is in a **selling trend** and moves up on a given day → on **resistance formation, short it**.
- If the market is in a **selling trend** and is falling → on **support formation, buy it** (it won't keep falling straight down without a bounce).

So the general tendency:
- **Falling market** → first thing in your mind should be **buying** (it will take support somewhere and go up).
- **Rising market** → first thing should be **resistance then retrace** (it will form resistance and go down).

### The expectation (weak vs. strong)
When you identify whether the first swing has strength or weakness, set your **expectation** accordingly:

- **Weak selling swing** → a small support forms → **intraday high breakout** is the general expectation (recovery). If it's a strong selling swing, then... `[unclear]`.
- **Weak buying swing** → **intraday low** — a reversal expectation.
- **Continuation** case → the market keeps going; if strong, target accordingly and you'll get it.
- In a **reversal pattern**, the minimum expectation is an `intraday high` / `intraday low` breakdown.

He stressed he wasn't asserting the "facts" strictly, but that the **minimum expectation** you keep should be these. He also said: as a trader, execute where the *probability is highest* — on the weak selling swing (support → intraday high), because that's what market nature most often does.

### Risk management / small SL
If you keep a small stop-loss you get a better **return on investment**. If your ratio is fixed but you try to **consistently capture very big momentum every single day**, your failure rate will be high. So keep the SL small and the expectation realistic — trade the highest-probability move.
