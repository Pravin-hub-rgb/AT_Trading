## What the "major overview" actually is

The whole idea of the major overview is to observe, at a large level, what is happening in the market. If you want, you can use the daily timeframe for this — observing on the daily frame keeps the detail, so you can see things better.

**Personal chart preference:** The instructor's personal choice is to use **5-minute charts zoomed out** (rather than daily), because the same data shows on daily candles but 5-minute has more detail, so you can observe things better.

**Data requirement:** When zooming out for major overview, you need at least **6–10 trading sessions visible** on the chart to properly observe the big picture. You can look at 8–10 sessions. The effort is to observe the major overview — where is the market within its major levels?

In the major overview, the question boils down to this: **where is the market trading within its major levels** — is it running at a high level, or has it performed at a lower level / moved into a range?

### Two-outcome framework at lower/upper levels

When market arrives at lower levels (or upper levels), exactly two things can happen:
1. **Bounce back** from support (or rejection from resistance)
2. **Continue lower** with lower highs (or continue higher with higher lows)

The market can't stay in the middle — it must pick a direction.

### When marking gets confusing — the mountain pattern
A confusion can arise when the market makes one big swing and then reverses — going up and then coming all the way back down in a single trading session. That creates a mountain: a big divergence between the buying side and the selling side. In that case you can consider the **mid-point** of that mountain as a candidate mark. If continuation happens from either side, you can mark those intermediate points too. So the idea is: you watch the big swings that get created at the market level, mark around them, and draw conclusions based on the relationship of the price to those ranges.

### Marking two ranges (detailed)
Once the major swings are marked, we compare — normally you'd base the comparison on **two ranges**:
1. The **recent range** (the current/nearest range the market is in) — this is generally the **smaller range**, marked from recent swing highs/lows.
2. The **next range** — the support/resistance we mark from the next, **bigger, older swings**.

For example, if I shift to the daily chart, the recent range gives a smaller box, and the next support/resistance is marked from the larger swings. He illustrated with a few recent sessions crowded into that recent range (across roughly the Feb sessions around the ~21st, 24th, 25th-26th, 29th, up to the most recent Friday, 1 March) — which is how you conclude which level the market is actually performing from on a given day. All range/support-resistance marking is done on the basis of buying/selling swings.

### Range updating on breakouts
As long as market moves within a smaller range, the overview is based on that range. Once it goes to all-time high or breaks any level, the **new range updates the smaller range**. The overview then shifts to the new range. So: mark the range → observe within it → when a level breaks, update the range → re-observe.

## Pace of momentum — the core mechanism

A lot of people have seen the tools — but how do you actually observe them? For example, how fib is plotted: it depends on the **pace of momentum**.

### What pace of momentum means
Pace of momentum = **speed of momentum**. At what pace is the market moving? How many candles does it take to cover a distance?

- **High pace of momentum:** The market moves fast — fewer candles, continuous movement without retracement. Sharp, fast, with stronger domination.
- **Low pace of momentum:** The market moves slow — more candles, more retracements along the way. The swing you got wasn't sharp.

So, a single buying swing — the market moved in a direction, but the swing you got wasn't sharp. Actually it can still be a buying swing — but the pace of momentum is slow. And in another case, the same kind of move can be set on a higher velocity — sharp, fast, with stronger domination.

### How pace determines swing marking
Whatever the pace of momentum, the swing marking is done on the basis of the swing itself — its start and end. When two references are marked (a major support and a major resistance), the more cases you plot against those levels, the better — because the market reacts to respect levels. Depending on the condition the market is in and how the swing was created, it responds to those respect levels.

### How pace connects to Fibonacci
Fibonacci is plotted from the **start of the momentum swing to where the momentum completes**. The pace of momentum determines where that completion point is. High pace = completion comes faster (fewer candles). Low pace = completion comes slower (more candles).

### Pace break triggers reversals
Reversals and continuations happen based on **pace of momentum break**, not just highs/lows break. When the pace of momentum changes (slows down or speeds up compared to the prior swing), that's when direction changes. The pace of momentum break is what triggers the market to change direction.

### Swings exist at all timeframes
Whether buying/selling swing is at **intraday level**, or at **weekly level**, or on zoomed-out big swings — the same rules apply. The concept is universal across timeframes.

## Which moves the market: supply and demand

Why does the market hold a level the way it does?

When you see the market rise and make higher lows, and each time it's bought at higher levels, that tells you **demand is higher** — people are willing to buy at higher levels. So price moves up.

When there's so much demand that a level wouldn't be tested the way it is... The market tests a support. If the buyers had that much strength, why would the price fall all the way to the support? The conclusion: when the price fell to a support level, **supply dominated at that point** — nobody was buying there. Over time people kept selling at lower levels, and ultimately supply stayed higher.

### The 100-quantity example
Suppose there are 100 quantities available and buyers are more than sellers. Someone will be willing to pay a higher price — there's a shortage. So people buy at higher prices, and price rises. That's why higher lows form: demand exceeds supply at each level.

But if price falls to a support level, it means **supply exceeded demand** at those higher prices. Nobody was willing to buy there, so price dropped until buyers showed up.

### Supply/demand and Indian markets
Indian market is regulated, so extreme behavior (like crypto-style crashes) is less common. But the general principle stays the same: price moves on the basis of supply-demand domination.

In summary, this is how price moves on the basis of domination / supply-and-demand. The market's general behavior: when support is tested, price doesn't suddenly jump from a major low straight to a major high — that's not the market's general tendency.

## Using the general tendency

We can't force the market to move the way we want; but there are times when we *can* use the market's nature to our advantage. Once the trend goes in a direction, the market tends to behave accordingly.

So, for example, when the market is at a lower level — in a strong selling zone — there's a reduced probability the market simply flips straight to the buying side. That's the kind of thing a trader uses.

### Specific rules for higher/lower levels
- **Market at all-time high or higher level:** If it starts falling, it won't fall continuously. It will make support, then bounce back. General tendency: from highs, retracement happens in steps (not straight down).
- **Market at lower level / support:** If it starts rising, it won't rise continuously. It will make resistance, then pull back. General tendency: from lows, recovery happens in steps (not straight up).
- **Continuous reversal is NOT expected** from either extreme. The market moves in swings — that's its nature.

The key framing: **most people lose money precisely because they fight the market's nature** — it keeps swinging down, then up, then down again. If instead you *use* that nature to your benefit, your whole game changes. This is NOT a strategy or trading system — it is **market understanding**, a perspective (nazariya). Make this nature your edge by executing in its favor.

So we take positions **in line with** the expected momentum (buying on support, selling at resistance), rather than fighting it.

## Trading plan components

A trading plan has three components:
1. **Entry execution methods** — how you enter trades
2. **Risk management** — how you manage the trade once in it
3. **Clarity of the decision** — knowing what to do when market opens, how it will react, what chart pattern to make, and what decision to take

**Clarity of decision is the most important.** First comes clarity of what to do in the market. When you know from the overview that market won't continuously fall, you wait for support confirmation calmly. That's the right execution mindset.

## Execution method & risk sizing

So beyond the identification and the overview, there's a whole **execution method** side to this.

If you're at the beginning / professionalism stage, you can plan a small entry. With a small entry comes a small stop-loss. If you want to take big targets with a small stop, do it — but keep the **quantity small**.

- If the trade goes **against** you, a big momentum could take more loss — but with a small quantity, the loss stays small.
- If it goes **in your favor**, the profit is smaller too — but that's fine. The key for a beginner is that the **mental pressure** stays low.

Because once mental pressure rises, discipline breaks, **emotion** takes over — and once emotion is in, "the job is over." Remember the saying: **whoever is scared, is already dead**. When you feel fear or emotion in the market, consider it a signal — exit the trade. It doesn't matter whether the target or stop-loss was going to be hit — it means your mental state isn't right for execution right now. First fix your system and your emotion, then execute — with a small quantity.

### Big SL + small quantity (NOT small SL)
Use a **larger stop loss with smaller quantity**, NOT small stop losses. The SL will be bigger — don't try to place tiny stops. With bigger SL and smaller quantity, the mental pressure stays manageable.

### Acceptable R:R range
If 1:3 or 1:4 isn't available, **exit at 1:2 or even 1:1.5** — if you don't get 1:4 or 1:3, exiting at 1:2 or 1:1.5 is completely fine. The goal is peace of mind, not maximizing R:R. The positive mindset from seeing green panels is more important than squeezing every last point.

### Positive mindset from high success rate
Better to execute in a method with **high success rate** — where you see green panels more often. Even if risk-reward is lower, the positive mindset from consistent wins builds confidence. You should be seeing green, green panels — that builds the mental state needed for trading.

## The backup (easiest) execution method

This connects to why there is a **backup system** — a setup that is "typically very easy to execute," specifically for that reason.

In this method, you **don't** trade the tricky small things prematurely. What are the "small things"? Planning a small stop-loss, a small target, a small risk-management scheme. All of that is complicated.

Instead — because you are taking *more* time to confirm, more observation, and only executing on a **proper confirmation** — the execution becomes straightforward and safe. This is an easy-to-execute method: wait for confirmation, execute on confirmed support. If support forms and the market holds, it's as good as a proper setup.

The catch with small stop-loss planning is: the entry has to be **precise**. If the entry isn't precise, then the momentum may not come right after the entry — and you're out.

For this style of trading, **option selling** is the preferred, easier method — because you give even more time for confirmation and can hold the position comfortably (he noted he'd rarely seen losses on this base method). Option *buying* on top of it is also fine, but it adds a bit more risk. Even in sideways markets, selling positions don't generate losses — premium decay works in your favor.

## Right mindset of execution

The right execution mindset = **knowing each and every step** — execution should be such that you know what is going to happen at every step. Not worrying about where the market will go.

From the overview, you know: market won't continuously fall. So you sit peacefully and observe. When market gives proper confirmation that support has formed, you buy in small quantity. This is how execution should feel — calm, methodical, not rushed.

## The backup plan overall
So this six-day live session has a **backup plan**: for anyone who isn't yet able to take the more complicated things we'll discuss later, there's a simple method they can execute profitably, and keep doing until they level up.

It's also about your mental peace: to be able to trade a process successfully, then later learn the deeper complications. So begin by getting this "proper process" working — trade it successfully and continuously — then, later, improve into the deeper concepts.

**Confidence threshold before advancing:** confidence is essential — once it arrives (after seeing the consistent losses stop and feeling that things are changing), then you can switch methods. Master the backup plan first, build confidence, then switch to advanced methods. Don't rush to complicated systems before the basics are solid.

## Option execution — buying vs. selling

The practical thread running through the session's walkthroughs was **options**, with a simple capital-size rule:

- If you have a **small capital**, do **option buying** — he personally would not go for option selling on small capital.
- If you have a **big capital** (where buying no longer scales well / you keep crossing limits), **option selling is the safer, preferred route** — because the base method is confirmation-based, the trade can be held longer, and a bigger stop fits.

So in practice: with small capital you buy (usually from the lower/support side); with big capital you sell the opposite side. Either way, the overview decides the side.

Walkthrough mechanics (on a selling read / market at the high side):
- If the market makes the **intraday high and doesn't hold it** — a **rejection candle** forms — you can **sell the call option**.
- After it falls and a **support forms**, you buy on confirmation (at the lower level). If the market then turns up (aggressive buying starts), **exit the selling-side lot**.
- If a **buying swing fails** — the buying swing you believed in breaks down — you can take a **put option in small quantity**, placing the stop near the swing.
- Where the market is just **sideways**, the call/put position keeps decaying, so you still make money on that side; once momentum shows, exit on the confirmation.

## Strength/weakness determination — the decision framework

This is the core swing analysis framework:

### After a selling swing:
1. **Wait for recovery** — let the market bounce back
2. **Check: does the recovery reach the intraday high?**
   - **Yes (V-shape recovery reaches intraday high):** This is a **sign of strength**. The market has buyers. Do NOT sell call options. Instead, buy put options or sell call options only after strength is confirmed to fail.
   - **No (recovery doesn't reach intraday high):** This is a **sign of weakness**. The market can't push back up. Sell call options.

### After a buying swing:
1. **Wait for retracement** — let the market pull back
2. **Check: does the retracement reach support?**
   - **Yes (retracement reaches support = weakness):** The buying swing failed. Sell.
   - **No (retracement doesn't reach support, forms higher = strength):** The buying swing held. Buy.

### Complete sequence: Support → Recovery → Intraday high = strength
Market falls → support forms → recovery with strength → reaches intraday high → this is a sign of strength. Don't wait for full breakout — **execute at 70–80% recovery**: when the recovery has reached 70–80%, sell the call option right there. Don't wait for the last 20%.

## Two decisions per session

In any trading session, there are exactly **two decisions** you can make:
1. **Retracement trade** — enter on the retracement in the direction of the swing
2. **Support-based trade** — after support forms, plan buying side

Both can be executed in the same session. The retracement trade is the morning-session based entry. The support-based trade is the overview-based entry.

## Handling both sides — the two-position approach

Later in the session he walked through a real day where both sides, buying and selling, were plausible, and showed a practical approach:

### Structural distinction: first position vs second position
- **First position** comes from **morning session analysis** (e.g., selling side based on morning swing)
- **Second position** comes from **overview/afternoon analysis** (e.g., buying side based on major overview)
- They serve different purposes and come from different analysis timeframes.

### The two-slot cut-and-hold system
- If you've set up positions on **both sides** (buying and selling), when one goes against you, **cut that one**; hold the one that aligns with the major overview.
- First slot cut = approximately **1:1 loss**. Second slot held until near the lows/highs.
- **Hold the overview-aligned position until near the lows** — when will we cut it? Only when price comes near the lows. Don't exit early; wait until price approaches the target level.

### When directions match — don't double
If the overview and morning session both say buy, take ONE position, not two. If they clash (one says buy, the other says sell), plan BOTH sides — the double-double position approach applies when the views conflict; when there's no clash, a single position suffices.

### Second position executes BEFORE first is cut
The second position (opposite side) executes based on the overview **before** the first losing position is formally cut — the second position gets executed because the overview says that if a rejection comes, short it. When the overview triggers, execute the second position; then cut the first one when it's clearly losing.

### Exact exit trigger for buying position
Exit the buying position (e.g., the call option) when the market starts falling with weakness and price approaches the intraday lows.

So in some days the market stays **sideways**. In a sideways market, selling makes you money for certain — the sold premium keeps decaying — and at the closing, if the market shoots the opposite side, the sufficient momentum comes and you book it.

In the **support strategy** — when the market is at support and confirms, you don't expect it to rocket straight up; you expect only a bounce first. When the market makes a buying swing and retraces, if it can't get back to the prior high (no recovery — meaning lack of strength), you either wait or act on the basis of what you've been taught.

The whole point: **placement** — observe the momentum, exit the side that is going against you, and hold the side that aligns with the overview.

## How V/W-shapes produce profit

Early in the walkthrough he pointed something out: the entire game runs because of the market's nature. If the market formed a selling swing first, then the buying side can target it. Using your own experience of thousands of sessions: if a selling swing forms first (sometimes four-five candles), it is most likely to **reverse** (form a V/W). The answer: yes, the selling swing's reversal is possible more often than not.

So:
- A selling swing forms → it reverses into a V/W-shape. Trading that, you make money.
- After that, the next W-shape also forms → continue, money again.
- If the reversal doesn't come (the first shape just keeps going) — still, money. Because in all of these cases the swing kept flowing in the direction.

### Swing reversal probability degrades
After a selling swing, V-shape reversal is likely. After **another** selling swing, the probability of another reversal drops significantly — successive reversals have diminishing probability. The first reversal is most reliable; each subsequent one is less likely.

### Where does the loss happen?
Only in one condition: if the market dips, goes up, dips again, doesn't break down further → weakness confirmed but no follow-through → then buying continuation happens. Otherwise, sideways = no loss from selling.

## M and W pattern identification

After buying swings, **M patterns** form → selling opportunities.
After selling swings, **W patterns** form → buying opportunities.

Loss happens when M is followed by continuation down, or W by continuation up. But these are the exception, not the rule.

## Stop-loss placement

Where will you place the SL? At the **intraday low** (for selling trades) or the **intraday high** (for buying trades). Don't weight a reversal all the way to the intraday extreme before acting — if the market reverses that far, your overview is wrong anyway.
