## What the "major overview" actually is

The whole idea of the major overview is to observe, at a large level, what is happening in the market. If you want, you can use the daily timeframe for this — observing on the daily frame keeps the detail, so you can see things better.

In the major overview, the question boils down to this: **where is the market trading within its major levels** — is it running at a high level, or has it performed at a lower level / moved into a range?

### When marking gets confusing
A confusion can arise when the market makes one big swing and then reverses — going up and then coming all the way back down in a single trading session. That creates a mountain: a big divergence between the buying side and the selling side. In that case you can consider recent points on either side as candidate marks. So the idea is: you watch the big swings that get created at the market level, mark around them, and draw conclusions based on the relationship of the price to those ranges.

### Marking two ranges
Once the major swings are marked, we compare — normally you'd base the comparison on **two ranges**:
1. The **recent range** (the current/nearest range the market is in), and
2. The **next range** — the support/resistance we mark from the next, bigger swings.

For example, if I shift to the daily chart, the recent range gives a smaller box, and the next support/resistance is marked from the larger swings. He illustrated with a few recent sessions crowded into that recent range (across roughly the Feb sessions around the ~21st, 24th, 25th-26th, 29th, up to the most recent Friday, 1 March) — which is how you conclude which level the market is actually performing from on a given day. All range/support-resistance marking is done on the basis of buying/selling swings.

### The pace of momentum
A lot of people have seen the tools — but how do you actually observe them? For example, how fib is plotted: it depends on the **pace of momentum**. What does pace of momentum mean? At what pace is the market moving?

So, a single buying swing — the market moved in a direction, but the swing you got wasn't sharp. Actually it can still be a buying swing — but the pace of momentum is slow. And in another case, the same kind of move can be set on a higher velocity — sharp, fast, with stronger domination.

It comes down to this: whatever the pace of momentum, the swing marking is done on the basis of the swing itself — its start and end. When two references are marked (a major support and a major resistance), the more cases you plot against those levels, the better — because the market reacts to respect levels. Depending on the condition the market is in and how the swing was created, it responds to those respect levels.

## Which moves the market: supply and demand

Why does the market hold a level the way it does?

When you see the market rise and make higher lows, and each time it's bought at higher levels, that tells you **demand is higher** — people are willing to buy at higher levels. So price moves up.

When there's so much demand that a level wouldn't be tested the way it is... The market tests a support. If the buyers had that much strength, why would the price fall all the way to the support? The conclusion: when the price fell to a support level, **supply dominated at that point** — nobody was buying there. Over time people kept selling at lower levels, and ultimately supply stayed higher.

So whenever a selling swing gets created, the question is why it could fall that far: it means at those prices supply exceeded demand. And when a buying swing holds and doesn't fall further, it means people were buying — demand was higher at that level.

Whatever the chart, its specific characteristics show this. And this connection to supply is a major reason — the product sounds `[unclear]` — but the general conclusion stays the same.

In summary, this is how price moves on the basis of domination / supply-and-demand. The market's general behavior: when support is tested, price doesn't suddenly jump from a major low straight to a major high — that's not the market's general tendency.

## Using the general tendency

We can't force the market to move the way we want; but there are times when we *can* use the market's nature to our advantage. Once the trend goes in a direction, the market tends to behave accordingly.

So, for example, when the market is at a lower level — in a strong selling zone — there's a reduced probability the market simply flips straight to the buying side. That's the kind of thing a trader uses.

The key framing: **most people lose money precisely because they fight the market's nature** — it keeps swinging down, then up, then down again. If instead you *use* that nature to your benefit, your whole game changes. So we take positions **in line with** the expected momentum (buying on support, selling at resistance), rather than fighting it.

## Execution method & risk sizing

So beyond the identification and the overview, there's a whole **execution method** side to this.

If you're at the beginning / professionalism stage, you can plan a small entry. With a small entry comes a small stop-loss. If you want to take big targets with a small stop, do it — but keep the **quantity small**.

- If the trade goes **against** you, a big momentum could take more loss — but with a small quantity, the loss stays small.
- If it goes **in your favor**, the profit is smaller too — but that's fine. The key for a beginner is that the **mental pressure** stays low.

Because once mental pressure rises, discipline breaks, **emotion** takes over — and once emotion is in, "the job is over." Remember the saying: **whoever is scared, is already dead**. When you feel fear or emotion in the market, consider it a signal — exit the trade. It doesn't matter whether the target or stop-loss was going to be hit — it means your mental state isn't right for execution right now. First fix your system and your emotion, then execute — with a small quantity.

## The backup (easiest) execution method

This connects to why there is a **backup system** — a setup that is "typically very easy to execute," specifically for that reason.

In this method, you **don't** trade the tricky small things prematurely. What are the "small things"? Planning a small stop-loss, a small target, a small risk-management scheme. All of that is complicated.

Instead — because you are taking *more* time to confirm, more observation, and only executing on a **proper confirmation** — the execution becomes straightforward and safe. This is an easy-to-execute method: wait for confirmation, execute on confirmed support. If support forms and the market holds, it's as good as a proper setup.

The catch with small stop-loss planning is: the entry has to be **precise**. If the entry isn't precise, then the momentum may not come right after the entry — and you're out.

For this style of trading, **option selling** is the preferred, easier method — because you give even more time for confirmation and can hold the position comfortably (he noted he'd rarely seen losses on this base method). Option *buying* on top of it is also fine, but it adds a bit more risk.

## Option execution — buying vs. selling

The practical thread running through the session's walkthroughs was **options**, with a simple capital-size rule:

- If you have a **small capital**, do **option buying** — he personally would not go for option selling on small capital.
- If you have a **big capital** (where buying no longer scales well / you keep crossing limits), **option selling is the safer, preferred route** — because the base method is confirmation-based, the trade can be held longer, and a bigger stop fits.

So in practice: with small capital you buy (usually from the lower/support side); with big capital you sell the opposite side. Either way, the overview decides the side.

Walkthrough mechanics (on a selling read / market at the high side):
- If the market makes the **intraday high and doesn't hold it** — a **rejection candle** forms — you can **sell the call option**.
- After it falls and a **support forms**, you buy on confirmation (at the lower level). If the market then turns up (aggressive buying starts), **exit the selling-side lot**.
- If a **buying swing fails** — the buying swing you believed in breaks down — you can take a **put option in small quantity**, placing the stop near the swing.
- Where the market is just **sideways**, the call/put position keeps decaying, so you still make money on that side; once momentum shows, exit on the confirmation.

## Handling both sides — the two-position approach

Later in the session he walked through a real day where both sides, buying and selling, were plausible, and showed a practical approach:

- If you've set up positions on **both sides** (buying and selling), when one goes against you, cut that one; hold the one that aligns with the major overview.
- Example: on a day when the market broke down and a big selling came, you might cut the selling position — while the buying-side position that aligns with the market overview is held.
- You keep that till it comes back to the intraday low — and when it does, you book it.
- Alternatively, when you're holding for recovery (e.g. buying side), if the recovery brings price all the way back to the intraday low, you can't give more. Let it run until it shows that.
- When both sides of momentum aren't coming, one is. In these cases, the two-position plan protects you.

So in some days the market stays **sideways**. In a sideways market, selling (or that side) makes you money — and at the closing, if the market shot the opposite side, the sufficient momentum comes and you book it.

In the **support strategy** — when the market is at support and confirms, you don't expect it to rocket straight up; you expect only a bounce first. When the market makes a buying swing and retraces, if it can't get back to the prior high (no recovery — meaning lack of strength), you either wait or act on the basis of what you've been taught.

The whole point: **placement** — observe the momentum, exit the side that is going against you, and hold the side that aligns with the overview.

## How V/W-shapes produce profit

Early in the walkthrough he pointed something out: the entire game runs because of the market's nature. If the market formed a selling swing first, then the buying side can target it. Using your own experience of thousands of sessions: if a selling swing forms first (sometimes four-five candles), it is most likely to **reverse** (form a V/W). The answer: yes, the selling swing's reversal is possible more often than not.

So:
- A selling swing forms → it reverses into a V/W-shape. Trading that, you make money.
- After that, the next W-shape also forms → continue, money again.
- If the reversal doesn't come (the first shape just keeps going) — still, money. Because in all of these cases the swing kept flowing in the direction.

Where does the **loss** happen? Only in one condition: if the market dips, goes down, retraces, goes back down to a level, and even after the strength is confirmed and the intraday low gets tested — the market doesn't recover properly — only then the loss happens.
